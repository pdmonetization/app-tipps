A generated quiz is useful when it makes you retrieve an answer before you see it. It is less useful when you click through a large question bank without checking whether the questions reflect your course material. Start with a small topic, then use mistakes to decide what to study next.

This is a researched guide checked on October 6, 2026, with original practice examples. It does not claim measured learning improvements or hands-on testing. If you have not set up a notebook, start with our [mobile beginner guide](/gemini-notebook-mobile-first-notebook/).

## Generate the study aid

Add your material and open Studio. Google's [flashcard and quiz instructions](https://support.google.com/gemininotebook/answer/16958963?hl=en) describe selecting Flashcards or Quizzes, or using the customization control first. You need edit access to generate or delete them. Options include difficulty and a prompt describing the topic or audience. Generated cards and quizzes have explanation and review controls.

Google has also [documented flashcards and quizzes on mobile](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-labs/notebooklm-app-quizzes-flashcards/). If your phone does not show the expected control, check the app version and account, then inspect the web version rather than assuming the feature requires payment.

## Select a manageable slice

For a biology chapter, separate “cell structures” from “cell division.” A missed question about a mitochondrion should lead you back to that topic, not trigger another twenty questions across an entire textbook.

Try this customization request: “Use only the selected cell-structure notes. Ask about functions and differences between structures. Avoid material that does not appear in those notes.” It is a suggested prompt, not a verified guarantee of source coverage.

Before studying, inspect a few items. Is the answer supported by the material? Is the question understandable without hidden context? Does it test something you actually need to know?

## Use this original practice note

> Evaporation changes liquid water into water vapour. Condensation changes water vapour into liquid water. Precipitation includes rain, snow and hail. These processes are parts of the water cycle.

A useful flashcard asks “Which process changes water vapour into liquid water?” and expects condensation. A useful comparison question asks how evaporation differs from condensation. A poor question asks for an exact global evaporation rate: the note contains no such figure.

You can paste this short note as a practice source before uploading your own material. The expected answers above are an editorial exercise, not a transcript of generated cards.

## A ten-minute revision routine

1. Spend two minutes answering a handful of cards without revealing the answers.
2. Spend three minutes checking missed answers against the original material.
3. Write one sentence explaining each mistake in your own words.
4. Spend three minutes attempting the missed concepts again, preferably with different wording.
5. Use the remaining time to mark the passage you should reread later.

This is a proposed routine, not a scientifically validated timing prescription. Adjust the session to the subject and your concentration.

## Choose flashcards or a quiz

| Your goal | Starting format |
| --- | --- |
| Recall terms, dates or definitions | Flashcards |
| Distinguish similar ideas | Quiz with explanations |
| Explain a multi-step process | Answer aloud, then check the source |
| Understand why an answer is wrong | Read the explanation and supporting passage |

A correct multiple-choice selection may be a guess. Try explaining why the alternatives are wrong before counting the topic as understood.
